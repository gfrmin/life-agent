# Nix for documents: a feasible but unbuilt system

> **Status: commissioned research input (2026-05), not a mandate.** Its verdicts informed
> ROADMAP decisions (Hamilton: no; DuckDB `vss`/`fts`: yes) but bind nothing — research reports
> are candidate inputs, never specs ([`PRINCIPLES.md`](../PRINCIPLES.md) §9).

**The system you want to build does not exist, and that is the interesting finding.** Every piece — content-addressed stores (Bazel REAPI, Nix CA derivations), demand-driven memoisation (Salsa, Adapton), semantic operators over unstructured data (LOTUS, Palimpzest, DocETL), typed LLM modules (DSPy), deterministic inference (Thinking Machines' batch-invariant kernels) — is mature or at least research-grade. No one has composed them. The closest attempt at personal scale is a two-folder markdown convention Karpathy published in April 2026. At enterprise scale, RisingWave is the only shipping system that literally materialises LLM outputs inside maintained views, and it only handles embeddings. This is a real design-space gap, not a saturated market. Against that gap, the risk is not technical infeasibility; it is building a cathedral when Karpathy's two-folder barn answers 80% of queries at 1% of the engineering cost. The rest of this report maps what is genuinely available off the shelf, where the theoretical foundations are strong enough to build on, and where a minimum viable version can start this weekend.

## The conceptual landscape is richer than it looks

The vision — transforms as pure functions of `(input, prompt, model)` producing content-addressed derivations, with demand-driven materialisation and cascading invalidation — sits at a precise point in **Mokhov, Mitchell & Peyton Jones' "Build Systems à la Carte" taxonomy**: a **suspending scheduler** (because LLM steps discover new dependencies dynamically — a RAG prompt may pull additional docs mid-call) combined with **constructive traces** (store output values, not just "did it run" bits, so downstream consumers can reuse). This is the "Cloud Shake" quadrant the authors explicitly flagged as desirable but underexplored. Nix sits in *deep constructive trace + restarting*; Bazel in *constructive trace + restarting*; what you want is their cousin with early cutoff and lazy expansion.

On the semantic side, four academic systems converge on the abstraction "LLM transforms as typed relational operators" but target batch analytics rather than personal-scale interactive use. **LOTUS** (Stanford/Berkeley, VLDB 2025, arXiv 2407.11418) exposes `sem_map`, `sem_filter`, `sem_join`, `sem_agg`, `sem_topk` over pandas-like dataframes, with a **cost model that swaps a "gold algorithm" for cheaper LM cascades under statistical accuracy targets** — reported 1000× speedups for filter/join/topk. **Palimpzest** (MIT, CIDR 2025, arXiv 2405.14696) centres on `Dataset(source, schema)` plus a `convert(schema_B)` primitive where missing typed fields are populated by LLM or synthesised code; its Abacus optimiser (arXiv 2505.14661) does cost-based plan selection across models and prompt strategies. **DocETL** (Berkeley EPIC, arXiv 2410.12189) is the most operationally complete: YAML pipelines with `map`, `reduce` (with scratchpad state for incremental folds), `resolve` (entity canonicalisation via embedding blocking + Union-Find), `split`/`gather` (context-preserving chunking), `gleaning` (iterative validator→refine loops), and a **persistent per-op disk cache at `~/.cache/docetl/`** keyed on op + inputs. **DSPy** (ICLR 2024) is orthogonal — it does not do relational semantics but turns each LLM call into a typed `Signature` + `Module` whose prompt and few-shots can be *compiled* (MIPROv2, GEPA) against a metric. DSPy modules are the right unit for the transform body; LOTUS/DocETL operators are the right algebra around them.

**The HKUST Data Agents survey** (arXiv 2510.23587, Oct 2025) positions all of these on an L0–L5 autonomy hierarchy inspired by SAE-J3016; a personal DAG-based KM system sits at L2/proto-L3 (tool-using to workflow-orchestrating). This survey plus its [awesome-data-agents](https://github.com/HKUSTDial/awesome-data-agents) companion is the canonical reading list. **Aryn/Sycamore** (arXiv 2409.00847) ships the only production-grade DocSet abstraction (hierarchical document → elements → chunks → properties with lineage) but assumes Ray scale.

**The critical technical finding is on determinism.** Hongyu He and the Thinking Machines Lab's September 2025 essay "[Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)" refutes the folk-wisdom that floating-point + concurrency is the problem. **The actual culprit is batch-size-dependent reduction order in RMSNorm, matmul, and attention kernels**; dynamic batching means your single prompt is computed alongside others whose arrival is nondeterministic, giving different bit results and occasional tie-breaks that flip the sampled token even at `temperature=0`. Their open-source `thinking-machines-lab/batch-invariant-ops` demonstrated 1000 identical outputs from Qwen3-8B on vLLM at ~10–40% throughput cost; SGLang integrated it on 22 September 2025. Practical implication for your cache keys: **without batch-invariant kernels, `temperature=0` is not deterministic on Ollama, llama.cpp, or vLLM** (confirmed in Ollama issues #586 and #5321). You must either pin a deterministic inference stack, run batch-size=1, or rely purely on memoisation — which is fine, because a content-addressed cache hit is bit-deterministic regardless of what the model would have done on a miss.

## DAG orchestrator comparison: Hamilton is the right answer

Across DVC, Snakemake, Hamilton, Dagster, Kedro, Prefect, Flyte, Metaflow, Nextflow, Pachyderm, and the LLM-native set (Windmill, n8n, Flowise, Langflow, Dify), exactly one tool has out-of-the-box semantics matching "content-addressed caching of pure-function transforms with demand-driven materialisation."

| Tool | Cache key | Auto-hashes function body? | Demand-driven? | Weight | Nix-ness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hamilton** | `(node, code_version, dep_data_versions)`; `code_version` = SHA of function source; `data_version` = recursive hash via `@singledispatch` hashers | **Yes, automatically** (shallow) | **Yes** — `dr.execute(["target"])` walks upstream lazily | SQLite + files in `./.hamilton_cache/` | ★★★★★ |
| **Dagster SDAs** | `hash(code_version, input_data_versions)` per asset | Only if `code_version="..."` set manually — else run_id | Yes via AutomationCondition | Daemon + webserver + SQLite | ★★★★ |
| **DVC** | MD5 of declared `deps` + exact command | Only if script listed as `-d` dep | Yes (`dvc repro`) | Single CLI | ★★★★ |
| **Snakemake** | `mtime + params + input + env + code` since 7.8 | Yes (rule source) | Yes (pull-based) | File-oriented DSL | ★★★★ |
| **Prefect 3** | `INPUTS + TASK_SOURCE + flow_run_id` | Yes via `TASK_SOURCE` policy (shallow) | No (imperative) | Server + Postgres | ★★★ |
| **Flyte** | `(cache_version string + signature + inputs)` | No — manual string bump | No | K8s-first | ★★ |
| **Metaflow** | Content-addressed storage only | No | No (`run` always runs) | Medium | ★★ |
| Windmill / n8n / Dify / Langflow / Flowise | TTL or none | No | No | Medium | ★ |

**[Hamilton](https://hamilton.apache.org/concepts/caching/)** is the only tool whose default cache model is literally what you described: a function DAG where each node's cache key is `(node_name, hash-of-function-source, hashes-of-dep-outputs)`, with pluggable `@singledispatch` hashers so you can teach it how to fingerprint Ollama responses or DuckDB relations, writing to `./.hamilton_cache/` with zero infrastructure. `dr.execute(["final_node"])` gives you demand-driven materialisation. The one caveat is that code-version hashing is **shallow** — it sees the decorated function but not nested helpers, so you either lift helpers into Hamilton nodes or bump a manual `@tag` when they change.

**Dagster's Software-Defined Assets** are the most *conceptually* aligned — asset versioning is built-in, the UI shows "Unsynced" labels for stale assets, and AutomationCondition policies implement eager/lazy materialisation. But it demands manual `code_version` discipline (the default is the run ID, which defeats the whole mechanism) and carries a daemon + webserver footprint that is heavy for a single-user system. Use Dagster only if you want the asset catalogue UI and are willing to bump version strings on every prompt edit.

**DVC** is the closest file-system analog to Nix and the right pick if your pipeline is "Podman container produces a file" rather than "Python function returns an object." The run-cache keyed on `(dep_hashes, exact command)` is genuinely content-addressed; the limitation is that it operates at shell-command granularity, not Python-function granularity, so refactors that leave the entry-point script unchanged will not invalidate.

**Dismiss the LLM-native visual tools (Langflow, Flowise, Dify, n8n, Windmill) for this purpose.** They solve orchestration of LLM apps, not reproducible content-addressed document pipelines; their caches are TTL-based or non-existent. Windmill's source-hash replay invalidation is interesting but aimed at durable execution (Temporal-style), not Nix-style reuse.

## A concrete storage and extraction stack that fits your box

**For document extraction, Docling is the long-term bet.** IBM's MIT-licensed [Docling](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.09869v4) produces a structured `DoclingDocument` preserving hierarchy across PDF, DOCX, PPTX, XLSX, HTML, images, audio, and LaTeX, with DocLayNet layout detection and TableFormer yielding ~98% accuracy on complex tables in recent benchmarks, running at ~0.5 s/page on an L4 GPU and usable on CPU. **MinerU** (AGPL-3.0) wins on scientific and CJK documents but carries a copyleft licence. **Marker** and **Surya** have the best handwriting OCR but ship under GPL. **Unstructured.io** is broadest but has a lower accuracy ceiling on complex tables. Route by document type: Docling default, MinerU for messy scientific PDFs, Tesseract or PaddleOCR-inside-Docling for plain OCR.

**For vector search, DuckDB `vss` + `fts` is correct for your scale and ethos.** At 11M words ≈ 150k–400k chunks of 512–1024 tokens, the HNSW index sits comfortably in RAM on any modern laptop, and the `fts` extension gives you real BM25 for hybrid search, joined to your metadata in pure SQL. Two sharp edges to design around: set `hnsw_enable_experimental_persistence = true` and back up the `.duckdb` file (no WAL recovery for custom indexes), and **filtered top-k is broken upstream** — `WHERE cat='X' ORDER BY distance LIMIT 10` returns fewer than 10 rows because HNSW runs before the filter. Either use the [`duckdb-hnsw-acorn` fork](https://github.com/cigrainger/duckdb-hnsw-acorn) (proper ACORN-1 filter pushdown) or over-fetch k·10 and filter in SQL. **LanceDB** is the natural graduation target if you cross ~2M chunks, also embedded, Arrow-native, with native hybrid query. **Do not add Qdrant, ChromaDB, or pgvector** — they introduce a second container and a sync pipeline to a design that explicitly chose single-file local-first.

**For the LLM framework layer, the right decomposition is LlamaIndex for ingestion + DSPy for prompts + nothing else.** LlamaIndex's [`IngestionPipeline`](https://developers.llamaindex.ai/python/framework/module_guides/loading/ingestion_pipeline/) hashes each `(node, transform)` pair and persists caches locally; its `docstore` handles `doc_id`+content-hash dedup. Critically, `pipeline.run()` is a plain Python callable — it plugs **into** your Hamilton DAG rather than wanting to own the orchestration. DSPy is orthogonal: use it offline to compile your classification, extraction, and answer-synthesis prompts against a 100–200-example eval set, running Llama-3.3 as the teacher and your smaller production model as the student; MIPROv2 reliably buys 10–30% quality without any code changes. **Haystack wants to own the pipeline** (its `Pipeline` is the DAG). **LangChain/LangGraph** adds little beyond what the above gives you; reserve LangGraph only if you later build a multi-agent chat UI on top. For knowledge graphs at personal scale, **Microsoft GraphRAG is cost-prohibitive** (hundreds of dollars to rebuild over 11M words even on cheap APIs, and rebuilds are non-incremental); **LightRAG** is the only GraphRAG variant with cheap incremental updates — defer until you actually need multi-hop entity queries, not before.

## The cache-key schema is the crux

The hardest design decision is what goes into the hash that keys an LLM-derived artifact. Drawing from DSPy's implementation, LangChain's `BaseCache` interface, Bazel's Action proto, and the Thinking Machines findings, a defensible schema for a personal pipeline is:

```
cache_key_v1 := blake3(canonical_json({
  schema_version:       1,
  input_cids:           [CIDv1 of each input, sorted],
  input_selectors:      {chunk ranges, slicing applied},
  prompt_template_cid:  CIDv1 of template source,
  prompt_template_vars: {canonicalised variable bindings},
  system_prompt_cid:    CIDv1 if separate,
  model_id:             "anthropic/claude-sonnet-4.5",
  model_version:        "20250929",           # pinned snapshot, never "latest"
  model_weights_hash:   sha256 of .gguf      # for local models
  tokenizer_id:         sha of tokenizer.json,
  inference_engine:     "vllm@0.6.3" | "ollama@0.4.1",
  temperature: 0.0, top_p, top_k, min_p, seed, max_tokens, stop_sequences,
  response_format:      {json_schema_cid},
  num_ctx:              8192,                 # required per Ollama issue #586
  batch_mode:           "single" | "batch_invariant",
  wrapper_version:      "my-pipeline@1.4.2",
  wrapper_code_cid:     CIDv1 of the function source,
  parser_version:       "json-v2"
}))
```

**Exclude** `api_key`, `api_base`, `request_id`, timestamps, latency, retry count — they destroy hit rates without improving correctness. The key design choices worth bolding: **pin model versions to dated snapshots, never `latest`** (providers silently roll models); **hash the tokenizer** when available (BPE drift changes prompt tokenisation for bitwise-identical text); **include the inference engine version** (batch-invariance kernels, sampler bug fixes, chat-template changes in llama.cpp all affect outputs); and **keep the prompt template and the rendered variables separate** so that editing the template invalidates all consumers while swapping the filled document only affects that one cache entry. Adopt **CIDv1 with BLAKE3 multihash** as the ID format — it is self-describing, supports future hash-function migration, and if you later want to share subsets via IPFS you are already interoperable.

Two pragmatic notes on provenance and semantic caching. **Emit W3C PROV-O JSON-LD sidecars** alongside cache entries: `Activity = llm_call`, `Agent = pinned model`, `Plan = prompt template`, `wasDerivedFrom` mirrors the CAS graph. The overhead is trivial and you get free audit trails. **Do not auto-serve semantic cache hits** — GPTCache and the LangChain `RedisSemanticCache` family return answers generated from a *different* input at some embedding distance, which breaks the pure-function abstraction, breaks PROV provenance, and breaks early cutoff. Semantic similarity is useful as an advisory layer ("here is a probably-similar past answer") but never as a substitute for the content-addressed path.

## Personal-KM reality check: nothing matches, and Karpathy's wiki is the baseline to beat

After surveying Khoj (34k★), Onyx (27k★), AnythingLLM, R2R, Open WebUI, Paperless-ngx, Quivr (37.8k★ but maintenance declining), Reor, and Obsidian Smart Connections, **none implement content-addressed, per-transform-hashed, DAG-cached derivation of LLM outputs.** They are applications that contain an ingestion pipeline, not reusable build systems. AnythingLLM is the closest in spirit — it keys its vector cache by UUID-v5 of file path and skips re-embedding of unchanged files — but the hash is on the filename, not content, and the logic is bound to its collector service. R2R's factory-pattern pipeline is the most philosophically aligned but runs as a service, not a reproducible build. What is **harvestable** from these projects are leaf-node components: **Megaparse** (Quivr's parser sub-project) as a clean parser drop-in, **Onyx's connector set** (best polished OSS connector code for Slack/GDrive/Confluence), and **Open WebUI's loader registry** as inspiration for your extractor dispatch.

On the pure-LLM-wiki side, Karpathy posted his "LLM Knowledge Base" idea on X on 2 April 2026 as a [gist](https://gist.github.com/karpathy/442a6bf555914893e9891c11519de94f) explicitly designed to be copy-pasted into Claude Code or Codex. The pattern is disarmingly simple: `raw/` (immutable human inputs, LLM read-only), `wiki/` (LLM-authored markdown with `[[wikilinks]]`, human read-only), and `CLAUDE.md` (co-evolved schema). The loop is ingest → compile → query (LLM reads the index plus 3–5 full pages) → lint for staleness and orphans → optionally file answers back as new pages. At ~100 articles and ~400k words Karpathy reports this answers complex questions without vector search. Several copycat implementations (Astro-Han/karpathy-llm-wiki, ScrapingArt/Karpathy-LLM-Wiki-Stack, NicholasSpisak/second-brain, rohitg00's "LLM Wiki v2" with typed KG) have appeared in the weeks since. The honest position: **Karpathy's wiki is your L1 baseline**, and it is the best existing evidence that users will read LLM-authored derived artifacts as primary material. What it cannot do is separate source-of-truth from cache, re-derive deterministically, or give you demand-driven views for questions not anticipated at compile time. Lex Fridman's riff (quoted in the VentureBeat coverage) on **ephemeral wikis spawned per research task** is essentially an admission that static wiki is the wrong granularity for many questions — which is the argument for your DAG approach.

The only shipping infrastructure pointing squarely at demand-driven materialised LLM views is **RisingWave's streaming-RAG pattern**, which puts `openai_embedding()` inside `CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW` with CDC-triggered re-embedding on document change. The transform is limited to embeddings, but it is the only production system in the market that treats LLM outputs as first-class maintained view rows. The closest hobby-scale prototype to your vision is `henrydaum/second-brain` — a "personal data warehouse that builds itself" with a four-stage pipeline and an agent that writes new tools at runtime — small, personal, but architecturally in the right neighbourhood.

## Recommended architecture and phased build

The synthesis: build a **typed Hamilton DAG over DuckDB** where each node is a pure function of `(input_CIDs, prompt_template_CID, model_snapshot, sampling_params, wrapper_code_CID)`; cache node outputs in a local CAS keyed by that tuple; run Ollama in batch-size=1 (or eventually a batch-invariant SGLang container) so the miss path is deterministic; use LlamaIndex's `IngestionPipeline` as a node for chunk-level transforms with its own per-component cache; use DSPy offline to compile prompts; and accept that for 80% of questions the **Karpathy-style compiled wiki is actually the best answer** — bake it in as the warm-cache layer, not as a competitor.

**Phase 0 (one weekend, no new code).** Clone Karpathy's gist. Point Claude Code or a similar agent at your 2,275 docs. Run the ingest → compile → lint → query loop. **Measure**: what fraction of your real questions are answered well? Record the failures — they define your actual DAG requirements. Skipping this step is the single biggest risk because the cathedral may not be worth building.

**Phase 1 (1–2 weekends).** Hamilton + DuckDB + Docling + Ollama + LlamaIndex `IngestionPipeline` end-to-end. Nodes: `load_raw → extract_text → chunk → embed → bm25_index → retrieve(question) → answer`. Hamilton's default `./.hamilton_cache/` handles caching. Use DuckDB `vss` + `fts` with the `duckdb-hnsw-acorn` fork for filtered queries. Everything runs on your Arch box in a Podman container. **Deliverable**: first query returned with citations, second query that reuses 90%+ of the cache.

**Phase 2 (2–3 weekends).** Add typed transforms beyond RAG: entity extraction (DSPy-compiled module), per-document summary, date extraction, cross-document entity resolution (borrow DocETL's `resolve` operator pattern — embedding-based blocking + Union-Find). Write a custom Hamilton hasher for your LLM response type that implements the cache-key schema above. Start persisting PROV-O sidecars per cache entry.

**Phase 3 (1 weekend when pain is real).** Demand-driven query planning: on a new question, have an LLM planner emit a small DAG of transforms to run against the cache-first store; materialise misses; cache results. This is where your vision diverges from Karpathy's and where the effort becomes justified.

**Phase 4 (optional, research-grade).** Swap the inference backend for SGLang in deterministic mode with Thinking Machines' batch-invariant ops. Migrate CAS IDs to CIDv1 with BLAKE3. Add LightRAG as a secondary index for multi-hop entity queries. Consider a `duckdb-datalog` or `differential-dataflow` layer only if query patterns genuinely require recursive views — they usually do not.

**Effort honesty check.** Phase 0 is hours. Phases 1–3 are roughly 40–80 hours of focused work for someone with your background. Phase 4 is open-ended. The ongoing maintenance burden is dominated not by your code but by two moving dependencies: **Docling's extractors** (bump every few months, verify accuracy) and **model snapshots** (when you upgrade, every cached LLM artifact produced with the old model remains valid but is frozen — design your invalidation UI around this, not around automatic re-derivation).

## Dependency risk and where the design could quietly rot

Hamilton is Apache-incubating and single-vendor-driven (DAGWorks); if velocity stalls, its cache semantics are small enough to re-implement over `diskcache` in a weekend — low lock-in. DuckDB `vss` is still labelled experimental for persistence; the backup plan is LanceDB, which costs a migration but nothing conceptual. LlamaIndex is the volatile framework in the stack — its APIs change quarterly — so wrap its `IngestionPipeline` in a thin adapter. Ollama is stable but its determinism is weak; the SGLang path exists if you ever need provable reproducibility. The deepest risk is **model snapshot churn on hosted APIs**: if you use Anthropic or OpenAI, your cache becomes a graveyard of old-model outputs every few months. For a personal system, pure local inference on pinned `.gguf` weights hashed into cache keys is both the privacy-respecting answer and the determinism answer.

## What remains genuinely uncertain

Three open questions survive this research. **First**, no academic or industry system has published on *incremental maintenance of LLM-derived views under document edits* — classical IVM (Enzyme on Spark, differential dataflow, DBSP) is SQL-aware but LLM-agnostic; LOTUS/DocETL/Palimpzest are batch-optimised and do not claim correctness under incremental updates. You will be inventing in this space, not adopting. **Second**, cross-hardware determinism is unresolved even with batch-invariant kernels; TML's demo is same-GPU, and PyTorch reproducibility docs explicitly do not guarantee determinism across architectures, so a pipeline that moves between a laptop and a workstation will see cache misses unless you include GPU architecture and CUDA version in the key. **Third**, the real question is economic rather than technical: for a personal corpus of 11M words, how often does the *fourth-order* derived view (a comparison of summaries of summaries of entity extractions) get reused enough to justify its cache slot? My suspicion, unfalsifiable without your query logs, is that the 80/20 is brutally tilted toward first- and second-order transforms, and that Karpathy's single-level compile captures most of the payoff. The DAG architecture earns its keep only if you discover you want to ask questions no static compile anticipates — which is precisely why Phase 0 is mandatory before any of this.

## Conclusion

The striking insight is not that this system is hard — it is that **every component is ready, documented, and open-source, and no one has assembled them**. Hamilton gives you Nix-style caching; LlamaIndex gives you the chunk-transform cache; DSPy gives you typed LLM modules; Docling gives you structured extraction; DuckDB gives you local-first storage with hybrid search; Thinking Machines gives you determinism; the "Build Systems à la Carte" taxonomy gives you the formal model; Karpathy gives you proof that the warm-cache layer works at 400k-word scale with two folders. The missing piece is taste: deciding which transforms to materialise eagerly, which lazily, and which to skip entirely in favour of just asking the LLM to re-read three source pages. That decision is the product, and it will be discovered by shipping Phase 0 this weekend, not by reading one more paper.
